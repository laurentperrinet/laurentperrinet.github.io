# [Modelling memory to understand the brain](https://laurentperrinet.github.io/slides/2026-11-03-grenoble/?transition=fade)
## *[Laurent U Perrinet](https://laurentperrinet.github.io/talk/2026-11-03-grenoble/)*
### <u>Seminar at Université Grenoble Alpes</u>

Contact me @ [laurent.perrinet@univ-amu.fr](mailto:laurent.perrinet@univ-amu.fr)

{{< speaker_note >}}
- thank the hosts; who I am (INT, Marseille)
- topic: models of neural networks storing patterns for seconds
- the problem: spiking neurons forget in tens of ms
- my answer: working memory in SNNs with chains of polychronous groups and heterogeneous delays
- slides + references on my web-site; first, why model memory?


*Hello*, and thanks for the invitation. I'm Laurent Perrinet from the Institut de Neurosciences de la Timone in Marseille. In this talk I will speak about modelling memory, that is, about neural networks that store patterns for durations of the order of seconds. This is a hard problem, as spiking neurons have a very short memory, of the order of tens of milliseconds. So how can one extend this memory to much larger durations? I will present a method for building *working memory in spiking neural networks, using chains of polychronous groups with heterogeneous delays*. These slides are available from my web-site, along with a number of references. But first, why is memory important? Let's start with a philosophical insight.


- time scale: the membrane time constant of cortical neurons is typically 10-20 ms, whereas working memory operates over seconds (Baddeley, 2003; Goldman-Rakic, 1995)
- polychronization: Izhikevich (2006), *Polychronization: computation with spikes*, Neural Computation 18(2):245-282

## Why Modelling Memory?

{{% fragment %}}{{< figure src="https://laurentperrinet.github.io/talk/2026-11-03-grenoble/featured.png" width="60%" >}}{{% /fragment %}}

{{< speaker_note >}}
**5 MINUTEs**

- living systems keep their order from birth to death, without collapsing
- Shannon: order/disorder is measurable in bits (entropy, mutual information)
- 2nd law: random collisions -> entropy grows (gas in a box with a hole)
- Maxwell's demon: a sorter that uses information to undo it = needs a memory
- the baby piling up blocks is a demon (and he pays the bill: he eats)
- Crick: the mind is the behaviour of nerve cells -> let's model them


Living systems are amazing: from birth to death, they do not collapse into disorder and maintain their structure and function over time. Yet quantifying this complexity is surprisingly simple: thanks to Claude Shannon, the complexity of a system can be measured by the information it holds, in bits, through entropy or mutual information, which are immensely useful in neuroscience. Following this formalism entropy increases, as demonstrated by the second law of thermodynamics, because of independent interactions.  This defines the arrow of time. Imagine a box with two compartments, one with gas, one without.  Now make a small hole between the two boxes: by random collisions the gas will go in the two compartments  and entropy increases. however there is only way to counteract it: the Maxwell demon. Imagine  that it sits at the entrance of this hole and sort for molecules of gas going from the the left compartment to the right, order will emerge again and entropy now decreases ! Knowing the dependencies across space and time is therefore what allows a system to disobey this law. Holding such information over time is what we will call memory, and it is the only way to escape this tendency to disorder. Here is a picture of such a Maxwell demon: this baby is able to pile up blocks. He knows, and that is why he is happy, that if he does not act, the second law will win and the blocks will collapse. As Francis Crick put it, "the human mind is the result of the behavior of nerve cells and their associated molecules": so let's model them.


- Shannon (1948), *A Mathematical Theory of Communication*, Bell System Technical Journal 27:379-423
- Maxwell's demon (1867); Szilard (1929) and Landauer (1961) link information to thermodynamics: erasing a bit of memory costs at least kT ln 2
- Crick (1994), *The Astonishing Hypothesis*
{{< /speaker_note >}}

## Modelling Memory: State-of-The-Art

{{< figure src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/7d/NN_LSTM-Cell_v2.svg" title="Long short-term memory ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_short-term_memory))" width="80%" >}}

{{< speaker_note >}}
**2 MINUTE**

- first family: recurrent networks, state fed back at the next step
- problem: vanishing/exploding gradient when learning through time
- LSTM: memory cell + gates (input, forget, output)
- works for speech, handwriting; but analog state, far from neurons


A first family of models is that of recurrent neural networks, where the state of the network at one time step is fed back at the next one. The problem is that, when learning, the gradient vanishes (or explodes) as it travels back in time, so long-range dependencies are hard to learn. The LSTM solves this with a memory cell and *gates*, input, forget and output, which decide what is written, kept or read. This made recurrent networks work for speech or handwriting, but the memory is a continuous, analog state, which is far from what neurons do.


- Hochreiter & Schmidhuber (1997), *Long short-term memory*, Neural Computation 9(8):1735-1780
- vanishing gradient: Hochreiter (1991, diploma thesis); Bengio, Simard & Frasconi (1994), IEEE Trans. Neural Networks 5(2):157-166
- forget gate added later: Gers, Schmidhuber & Cummins (2000), Neural Computation 12(10):2451-2471
- the cell state is a "constant error carousel": its self-connection is 1, so the gradient does not decay : todo give intuition
{{< /speaker_note >}}

## Modelling Memory: State-of-The-Art

{{< figure src="https://jalammar.github.io/images/t/transformer_resideual_layer_norm_2.png" title="The (Illustrated) Transformer ([Jay Alammar](https://jalammar.github.io/illustrated-transformer/))" width="60%" >}}

{{< speaker_note >}}
**2 MINUTE**

- breakthrough in NLP: the Transformer (the T in ChatGPT)
- no recurrence: attention compares the current token with the whole context
- long-range relationships used to predict the next token
- cost: memory = entire context window; massive, energy-hungry, not biological


A breakthrough happened in natural language processing with the Transformer architecture, the T in ChatGPT. There is no recurrence any more: *attention* compares the current token with all the tokens of the context window. It thus memorises long-range relationships and uses them on the current sequence, here to predict the next token. But the memory is the whole context window, read again at every step. This requires massive models, is energy-intensive, and is not biologically plausible.


- Vaswani et al. (2017), *Attention is all you need*, NeurIPS
- cost of self-attention grows quadratically with the context length n (n x n comparisons)
- Alammar's illustrated version: https://jalammar.github.io/illustrated-transformer/
- contrast for the talk: the brain keeps information in the *dynamics* of its network, not by re-reading a buffer
{{< /speaker_note >}}

## Spiking Neural Networks: Leaky Integrate-and-Fire

{{< figure src="https://github.com/SpikeAI/2022_polychronies-review/raw/c509f781ac0d6d874c92e54080d6f96a520c32a7/figures/LIF.gif" title="Review on Precise Spiking Motifs ([Grimaldi *et al*, 2023](https://laurentperrinet.github.io/publication/grimaldi-22-polychronies/))" width="80%" >}}

{{< speaker_note >}}
**2 MINUTE**

- a standard Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) neuron: the membrane potential integrates the input current and leaks back to rest
- when it crosses the threshold, the neuron emits a spike and is reset
- the leak sets the memory of a single neuron: the membrane time constant, of the order of 10 to 20 ms
- so how can a network of such neurons hold a pattern for seconds?
{{< /speaker_note >}}

## Spiking Neural Networks: Polychronization

{{< figure src="https://github.com/laurentperrinet/2023-07-20_HDSNN-ICANN/raw/master/figures/izhikevich_left.svg" width="100%" >}}

{{< speaker_note >}}
The core idea of the method follows the use of polychronous groups as defined by Izhikevich in 2006. Suppose three presynaptic neurons are connected to two postsynaptic neurons by certain weights and certain delays, which correspond to the time it takes for a spike to travel from one neuron to the next.

## Spiking Neural Networks: Polychronization

{{< figure src="https://github.com/SpikeAI/2022_polychronies-review/raw/c509f781ac0d6d874c92e54080d6f96a520c32a7/figures/HSD.gif" title="Review on Precise Spiking Motifs ([Grimaldi *et al*, 2023](https://laurentperrinet.github.io/publication/grimaldi-22-polychronies/))." width="80%" >}}

{{< speaker_note >}}
**2 MINUTE**

- a neuron with heterogeneous synaptic delays (HSD): each input reaches the soma after its own delay
- the delays shift the incoming spikes in time, so that a given temporal pattern arrives *synchronously* at the soma
- the neuron then acts as a detector of a precise spiking motif, not just of a firing rate
{{< /speaker_note >}}

## Spiking Neural Networks: Polychronization

{{< figure src="https://github.com/laurentperrinet/2023-07-20_HDSNN-ICANN/raw/master/figures/izhikevich_middle.svg" title="Polychronization: Computation with Spikes ([Izhikevich, 2006](https://www.izhikevich.org/publications/spnet.pdf))" width="100%" >}}

## Methods: chaining Polychronization

{{< figure src="https://github.com/SpikeAI/MNESIS/raw/4bdeec0834c2bee1edf2ede1aaa8ab6829ca3457/figures/izhikevich_rec.svg" title="Working Memory in a Recurrent Spiking Neural Networks ([LP, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.14096))" width="100%" >}}

{{< speaker_note >}}
Following on this idea - and similar to the original network from Izhikevich - one may build such a process in a recurrent network. Synapses are defined similarly, but act on the same population, not a separate one.

Given this architecture, and deviating now from Izhikevich, we may wish to define motifs such that given one context window (green shaded area), it predicts the occurrence of the spikes at the next time step. This allows to create a new context and a new prediction, such that we may chain these motifs and thereby sustain a pattern over durations much longer than the membrane time constant.
{{< /speaker_note >}}

---

## Methods: BPTT (snnTorch)

{{< figure src="https://github.com/SpikeAI/MNESIS/raw/4bdeec0834c2bee1edf2ede1aaa8ab6829ca3457/figures/unrolled.svg" title="Training SNNs Using Lessons From Deep Learning ([Eshraghian, 2023](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/10242251))" width="80%" >}}

{{< speaker_note >}}
We build an implementation of the network using snnTorch - and the delays add just another level of propagation in the unrolled computational graph - here represented by the delay line on the bottom. implementing a network of 1024 neurons with 41 delays and 16 different patterns

we define the task as repeating *exactly* all spikes from a randomly drawn target with a mean firing rate of 0.16 spikes per second per neuron (p_A = 1.6e-4 per 1 ms time step). the loss will be the F1-score, that is the harmonic mean between recall and precision. using a fastsigmoid surrogate gradient approximation, the network learns the targets in approximately 25 minutes (256 epochs)
{{< /speaker_note >}}

## Methods: leaky integrate-and-fire network

{{% fragment %}}
$$ I_j(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \bigl ( \sum_{d=1}^{D} \mathbf{W}_{j, i, d} \cdot s_i(t-d) \bigr ) $$
$$ u_j(t) = \beta \cdot u_j(t-1) \cdot (1 - s_j(t-1)) + I_j(t) $$
$$ s_j(t) = \mathbf{H}[u_j(t) \geq \vartheta] $$
{{% /fragment %}}
{{% fragment %}}
<small>$N$: number of neurons, $D$: number of delays (time steps of 1 ms), $\mathbf{W}_{j, i, d}$: weight from neuron $i$ to neuron $j$ with delay $d$, $s_i(t)$: spike (0 or 1) of neuron $i$, $I_j$: input current, $u_j$: membrane potential, $\beta$: membrane decay constant, $\vartheta$: firing threshold, $\mathbf{H}$: Heaviside step function.</small>
{{% /fragment %}}

{{< speaker_note >}}
- Each neuron integrates the spikes of *all* neurons over *all* the preceding $D$ time steps, weighted by the synaptic weights: the delays simply add one more dimension to the weights.
- the membrane potential decays by $\beta$ at each step and is reset after a spike: this is the discrete-time LIF neuron seen earlier
- a spike is emitted when the potential reaches the threshold $\vartheta$
{{< /speaker_note >}}

---

## Methods: weight initialisation

{{% fragment %}}
- $$ \mathbf{W} \mathbf{C} \approx \mathbf{S} $$
{{% /fragment %}}
{{% fragment %}}
$$ w_{j, i, d} = \frac{1}{N \cdot D \cdot p_A} \sum_{\mu=1}^{M} \sum_{t=D+1}^{T} s_i^{\mu}(t-d) \cdot \bigl( s_{j}^{\mu}(t) - \beta \cdot s_{j}^{\mu}(t-1) \bigr) $$
{{% /fragment %}}
{{% fragment %}}
<small>$\mathbf{S}$: target spikes, $\mathbf{C}$: context (spikes of the $D$ preceding time steps), $M$: number of target motifs (index $\mu$), $T$: duration of a motif (time steps), $p_A$: average spike probability per neuron and per time step, $\beta$: membrane decay constant.</small>
{{% /fragment %}}

{{< speaker_note >}}
However, convergence is quite slow, in particular because some places in the weight space may correspond to non-linear (dead or epileptic) regimes.

one may however use a weight initialisation. Indeed each prediction can be seen as a linear prediction of the next time step, and one may concatenate all these equations together and then invert them to get the weights using a Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse.

note that, written for each weight, this solution is a Hebbian-like rule (a correlation between the delayed pre-synaptic spikes and the deconvolved post-synaptic target), hence the reason why Hebbian-like learning may incidentally work for training this type of network.
{{< /speaker_note >}}

{{< video src="https://github.com/SpikeAI/MNESIS/raw/06c5537e11dbddb7508ae607a9490890739c4cfc/figures/TW.mp4" autoplay="yes" width="80%" loop="yes" >}}
